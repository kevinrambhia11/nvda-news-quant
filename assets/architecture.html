Start here — the system in 60 seconds

If you read only one section, read this one.

Every weekday evening, a program on one laptop wakes up, downloads the day's news and prices, and answers three questions. It has been graded on years of history for each one, and it is trusted very differently on each.

How wild will tomorrow be?

TRUSTED

The one question it answers well. Volatility is predictable because calm days cluster with calm days and wild days with wild days. This answer sets your position size: wilder forecast → smaller position, so your risk stays roughly constant instead of swinging with the market.

Will the move be unusually big?

ON TRIAL

A yes/no probability, and the one place news genuinely helps. Reading the actual news beats using price data alone by a small but repeated margin. Published daily and graded live before anyone relies on it.

Will the stock go up or down?

NOT TRUSTED

Nine different model designs all failed to beat simply holding the stock. This is what an efficient market looks like: by the time you read the news, the price already reflects it. Printed as context only.

What the desk will never do: place a trade, touch a broker, or move money. It produces a recommendation on a screen; a human decides everything. It is also a single-stock research instrument, not proven investment advice — see what's wrong with it before acting on anything here.

Why volatility instead of direction?

This surprises people, so it is worth stating plainly. If a model could reliably predict direction, that model would print money, and enough professionals hunt for it that any easy version is already gone — the price moves the instant news lands, long before a laptop in Mumbai reads it. Volatility is different: nobody gets rich knowing a stock will move 3% without knowing which way, so the pattern survives. That is why the desk's trusted product is a risk forecast, not a price forecast.

A day in the life — what actually happens, and when

All times India Standard Time. The US market opens at 19:00 IST during daylight-saving months, so everything below finishes before the bell.

EVERY 30 MIN
headline logger Saves a timestamped copy of headlines from Reuters, Bloomberg, BBC, FT, CNBC and others. This builds the desk's own news history for future research; it does not feed today's models.
17:00
step 1 · news top-up Downloads the article days missing from the archive (usually one day, a few hundred megabytes of database quota), removes duplicates, and converts the new headlines into numbers. If the download fails, the system continues on yesterday's news and says so — it never skips a day silently.
17:01
step 2 · grade yesterday Before predicting anything new, it looks up what the stock actually did and scores its own previous forecast: was the direction right, was the big-move call right, how did the realized move compare to the forecast volatility? Appended permanently to a learning log.
17:02
step 3 · apply the brain The trained neural network reads every article attached to the upcoming session and assigns each one a significance weight. No learning happens here — it is applying judgment it already has, the way a trained radiologist reads a new scan without re-attending medical school.
17:03
step 4 · predict Builds one row of about 53 numbers describing the world as of this moment, runs it through the three trained models, and writes the desk output: position size, loss limits, probability of a big move, advisory direction.
17:04
step 5 · publish Saves the forecast to a permanent archive and pushes it online, which updates the hosted dashboard. The archive is what the track record later grades — forecasts are timestamped before the market opens, so they cannot be quietly revised afterwards.
FRI 15:00
weekly re-education The only time anything learns. The brain retrains on all history, every model re-competes for its job in a tournament, and reports regenerate. Finishes about an hour before Friday's own signal, which therefore runs on fresh models.

STAGE 3FEATURES — the numbers a model may look at

A model cannot read a newspaper; it reads a row of numbers. Each row describes one trading day. There is exactly one rule governing what may go in it, and everything else in the system depends on it holding.

The iron rule. The row for trading day D may only contain news published through the end of day D−1 and prices through the previous close. The thing being predicted is the move from D's opening price to the next day's opening price. In other words: every number in the row is something you genuinely could have known before the opening bell. This makes cheating structurally impossible rather than merely forbidden — the same code builds the historical training rows and today's live row, so if it could see the future in testing, it would have to see the future live too, which is impossible.

Market features

Recent returns, trend distance from moving averages, a momentum gauge, realized volatility over several windows, drawdown depth, plus how the wider Nasdaq behaved. About 20 numbers.

Event features

Days until and since earnings, whether an earnings report lands inside tonight's holding window, and whether today is the morning after one.

News tone features

How positive coverage was, how unusual that is versus the last 60 days, and how much was written versus normal — the "loudness" signal.

Article-level features

THE NEWS EDGE

Eleven numbers distilled from the individual headlines: impact-weighted tone, disagreement between same-day articles, novelty versus the trailing month, and volume anomalies per category.

News memory (decay)

NEW

Ten more numbers: the same article signals carried forward with a fading half-life of 3 and 10 days, so a big story keeps influencing the desk for a week or more instead of vanishing overnight. Explained below.

Brain features

Five numbers out of the neural network: its direction and size readings for the day, how much the articles disagreed, and whether attention concentrated on one story or spread thin.

Why "news memory" was added

Until recently each article influenced exactly one trading day and then disappeared completely — a pulse. That is clearly wrong about how markets digest news: a major export-control announcement matters for days, and earnings drift on for a week or more. The decay layer fixes it by accumulating news with a half-life, so yesterday's story still counts at half weight, the day before at a quarter, and so on. It was added as a head-to-head competitor against the old pulse version, and it won in all three tournaments — the strongest single vote for the idea being right.

one leak-free row per trading day
STAGE 4THE TOURNAMENT — where trust is earned

Nothing here gets promoted by argument or intuition. Rival model designs compete on data their feature-builders never saw, and the winner's real grade comes from a stretch of history nothing was ever tuned against. Every Friday, every seat is re-contested from scratch — a model cannot keep its job by seniority.

Volatility — how big is tomorrow?

PRODUCT

A deliberately simple linear formula blending yesterday's, last week's and last month's realized movement, plus earnings flags. Explains 30% of next-day and 42% of next-week variation on untouched data. Fancier machine learning and news features both challenged it and lost.

Magnitude — bigger than normal?

ON TRIAL

Decision trees estimating the chance tomorrow's move exceeds its own recent typical size. Reading the news lifts accuracy from 0.533 to 0.568 (see the glossary for what those mean), and the probabilities are calibrated so a stated 60% really means 60%.

Direction — up or down?

ADVISORY

Nine designs, four different toolchains, including the neural network and the news memory. Best honest result: Sharpe 0.92 against 1.27 for simply holding the stock. Shown for context, never trusted with sizing.

How a walk-forward test works

A naive backtest trains on all history then tests on the same history, which is like marking your own exam with the answer sheet open. Instead, this desk marches through time: train on everything up to a date, predict the next month blindly, step forward, retrain, repeat. Every prediction ever scored was made by a model that had not yet seen the answer. The extra safeguard is a one-day gap at each retraining point, because the most recent day's outcome is not actually known at the moment a real signal would be generated.

only the trusted model sizes risk

Inside the brain — the neural network, plainly

The part most people find mysterious. It is simpler than it sounds.

A neural network is a machine with a few hundred thousand adjustable dials. Numbers go in, numbers come out, and the dials decide how. Training means showing it an example, measuring how wrong the output was, then nudging every dial a fraction in the direction that would have been less wrong — repeated hundreds of thousands of times until the dials settle into a configuration that captures real patterns. There is no understanding involved, just a very flexible function shaped by experience.

This desk uses two, doing completely different jobs.

MiniLM — the reader

BORROWED, FROZEN

Trained by others on billions of sentences; we never change it. Its only job is translation: hand it a headline, it returns 384 numbers — coordinates on a map of meaning where similar meanings land close together. "Nvidia beats earnings" and "NVDA tops estimates" share almost no words but end up nearly on top of each other. This is what makes the vector database a map rather than a keyword index.

NewsNet — the judge

TRAINED BY US

Never sees text, only MiniLM's coordinates plus which outlet published it and which category it belongs to. Its job is the one you actually care about: given roughly 180 articles today, which ones matter, and what do they imply?

Attention — the idea worth understanding

This answers the hardest question anyone asks of a news model: what happens when three or four articles on the same day contradict each other?

The naive answer is to average them. Attention does something better. For every article the network computes an importance score, then squashes all the scores so they sum to exactly 1. It is allocating a fixed budget of attention across the day's news — if one story deserves 31%, everything else must share the remaining 69%. Then it blends all the articles using those weights into a single summary of the day.

So conflict is handled twice over. The brain may simply not listen equally — one Reuters export-control story can outweigh a hundred syndicated reposts. And the disagreement itself becomes a measurement the downstream models can use: one number records how far apart the articles' implications were, another whether attention concentrated on a single story or smeared across many. A day with one loud story and a day with a hundred quiet ones look completely different to the desk.

How it was taught, and when it stops

It saw 1,923 trading days. For each: guess, compare against what the stock actually did next session, nudge the dials. One pass over all days is called an epoch. After every epoch it is tested on 213 recent days it never trained on, and the moment those scores stop improving, training halts — that is the signal it has begun memorizing quirks instead of learning patterns. In the latest run it stopped at epoch 9, which is early, and honestly tells you the genuine signal in headline text is thin.

The honest scorecard on the brain. It does exactly what it was designed to do and it makes the system's reasoning visible — you can read which stories it weighted and which outlets it learned to respect. But on measured accuracy it does not beat plain old ridge regression on the same inputs, and an earlier apparent edge turned out to be a data-alignment bug that has since been fixed. Its current value is transparency more than predictive power. The tournament keeps testing it every Friday; if the live record ever shows the attention weights adding real value, it gets promoted on evidence.

Models may only ever learn from history to their left. The news scorers train on the scorer training years. Every candidate is then compared on the clean judging window, which those scorers never saw — without this, a model that had memorized its own training era would post spectacular-looking numbers and win on a mirage. The crowned winner's real grade comes from the untouched holdout. All these boundaries are fixed calendar dates, so weekly retraining cannot quietly nibble the holdout away.

Four more safeguards worth knowing about:

Standard errors are printed next to every score

A Sharpe ratio measured over 430 days carries roughly ±0.77 of uncertainty. That means a candidate scoring 1.6 versus another at 1.3 is not meaningfully better — the gap is smaller than the measurement error. Printing the error bar next to every number makes false victories obvious instead of exciting.

The winner's curse, and why the tournament is suspicious of its own winner

Run nine coin-flipping models and the luckiest will look skilled. With nine candidates and that much noise, the expected best score under pure chance is around 1.1 — so a winner scoring 1.6 is barely outside what luck alone produces. This is exactly why no candidate is trusted on its selection score, only on the untouched holdout, and why the direction model remains advisory despite "winning".

Calibration — making probabilities mean what they say

A model can rank days correctly while being systematically overconfident, saying "80%" when it means 60%. Because a position size derived from a probability needs the number to be literally true, the magnitude head's output passes through a correction fitted on honest out-of-sample predictions. Raw score 0.276 improves to 0.251 after correction (lower is better).

The one test that cannot be gamed

Every historical safeguard above has a limit: the holdout has now been read around forty times across this project's experiments, which slowly turns it into a second selection window. The remedy is the live paper trail — forecasts timestamped and published before the market opens, graded afterwards, with no possibility of revision. The dashboard's Track Record tab is that scoreboard, and it is the only evidence immune to hindsight.

What's wrong with it — read before acting

From a four-lens independent audit. These are not bugs; they are the honest limits of what this design can prove.

It has not actually beaten buy-and-hold

On the untouched holdout the flagship leg returns 44.6% annually with a Sharpe of 1.29, against 63.7% and 1.26 for simply holding NVDA. The risk-adjusted edge of 0.03 is far smaller than its own measurement error, and it costs 19 percentage points of return to obtain. What survives honestly is a genuinely shallower drawdown (−30% versus −43%). A static "hold 75% and keep 25% in cash" would achieve something similar with no effort and no tax churn — a comparison the desk should run on itself and currently does not.

Everything was validated in one stock, one regime

All conclusions come from NVDA over a single AI bull market, on a ticker chosen precisely because it became the dominant AI story. Any long-tilted rule looks good in that window, and the drawdown benefit has never been observed in a regime where volatility spikes and the stock does not recover. Treat every number as descriptive of NVDA 2023–2026, not as a law.

The holdout is spent, so recent findings are suggestive only

Around forty separate evaluations have now been read off the same stretch of history, and decisions were taken after reading them. Both surviving positive results are about the size that pure multiple-comparisons noise produces. This is why the live track record matters more than any backtest number on this page.

The volatility model has a rival it is not allowed to lose to

A zero-parameter industry-standard smoothing formula actually beats the crowned model on the desk's own selection metric at both horizons, but analytic baselines are structurally barred from winning the seat. Separately, the model forecasts intraday range while sizing needs total risk including overnight gaps — which is exactly where news for this stock lands. Both are open items.

Practical gaps for a real operator
  • No capital base is configured, so the loss limits are quoted per million dollars rather than on real money.
  • No written stop-trusting rule: nothing defines the drawdown or error rate at which a human should switch the system off.
  • Indian tax treatment of frequent US-equity turnover is unmodelled and would plausibly erase a thin edge entirely. Currency conversion, remittance charges and idle-cash drag are likewise unpriced.
  • Two hours pass between the signal and the market open, and macro releases land in that window with no defined protocol for what to do about it.
  • Silent failure is possible: one recent evening's signal simply did not run, and nothing alerted anyone.
The news data is thinner than 710,000 headlines suggests

Roughly 58% of the archive is unique, English-leaning, multi-word headline text; the rest is identifier junk, non-English, or syndicated duplicates. More importantly the models read URL slugs rather than full articles, and about two-thirds of NVDA-category slugs contain no NVDA-related word at all. So the measured news edge may partly reflect attention and news-flow patterns rather than the meaning of specific stories — a humbler claim, still a useful one.

Glossary — every term on this page

Alphabetical within each group. If a word on this page confused you, it should be here.

Risk and money

VolatilityHow much a price moves, regardless of direction. Usually quoted per day or annualized. High volatility means big swings either way.
AnnualizedA daily figure scaled to a yearly rate, so numbers are comparable. A 2% daily move is roughly 32% annualized.
Target-vol sizingHolding a smaller position when forecast volatility is high and a larger one when it is low, so your risk stays constant instead of swinging with the market. The desk's core mechanism.
VaR (value at risk)A loss threshold at a confidence level. "95% VaR of $47,820" means on the worst 1 day in 20 you would lose about that much or more.
DrawdownThe peak-to-trough fall in an account's value. The most emotionally relevant risk number, because it is what you actually live through.
Sharpe ratioReturn divided by volatility — reward per unit of risk. Above 1 is respectable. Crucially, it is measured with error: over three years that error is roughly ±0.6.
Buy-and-holdJust owning the stock and doing nothing. The benchmark every strategy must beat to justify its existence, and the one this desk has not yet cleared.
ExposureThe share of time, or fraction of capital, actually invested. Lower exposure usually means lower return and lower risk.
Open-to-open returnThe change from one morning's opening price to the next. The desk uses this because it is what you actually capture by trading at the open on an evening signal.
Turnover / basis pointTurnover is how much you trade; a basis point is one hundredth of a percent. The backtest charges 5 basis points per position change to represent trading costs.

Models and machine learning

FeatureOne input number describing a day — yesterday's return, today's news tone, days until earnings. A model sees only features, never the world.
Training vs inferenceTraining adjusts a model's internal settings from examples. Inference applies an already-trained model to new data. This desk trains weekly and infers daily.
Linear regressionThe simplest model: multiply each input by a weight and add them up. The volatility winner is one of these, and simplicity is why it generalizes so well.
HARThe specific volatility recipe: blend yesterday's, last week's and last month's realized movement. A finance-standard approach that repeatedly beats fancier alternatives.
Gradient-boosted treesHundreds of small yes/no decision trees, each correcting the previous one's mistakes. Powerful on tabular data, used for the direction and magnitude heads.
Ridge regressionLinear regression with a penalty that discourages extreme weights, which keeps it stable when inputs are numerous and noisy. The article impact scorers are ridge models — and they beat the neural network.
EmbeddingA list of numbers representing a piece of text's meaning (here 384 of them). Similar meanings produce nearby lists, which is what allows mathematical comparison of headlines.
AttentionA mechanism that assigns each item in a group an importance weight, with all weights summing to 1. It is how the brain decides which of today's articles to listen to.
Epoch / early stoppingOne epoch is a full pass over the training examples. Early stopping halts training when performance on held-back data stops improving, preventing memorization.
Calibration (isotonic)Adjusting a model's probabilities so they mean what they claim — days labelled 60% should happen about 60% of the time. Isotonic is the specific order-preserving method used here.
AUCRanking accuracy from 0.5 (coin flip) to 1.0 (perfect). The magnitude head's 0.568 is a real but modest edge; the direction model's 0.491 is literally worse than a coin flip.
Brier scoreAverage squared error of probabilities — lower is better, 0.25 is what pure guessing scores. It punishes confident wrong answers, which is why calibration matters.
QLIKE / R²QLIKE scores volatility forecasts (lower is better). R² is the share of variation explained: the volatility model's 0.30 means it accounts for about 30% of what happens.
Half-life / decay (EWMA)How long news keeps mattering. A 3-day half-life means a story retains half its weight after 3 days, a quarter after 6. EWMA is the exponentially-weighted average that implements it.
Pulse vs decayPulse: news affects exactly one day then vanishes. Decay: news fades gradually. The desk now tests both against each other, and decay is winning.

Honesty and testing

Walk-forwardTesting by marching through time — train, predict the next stretch blindly, step forward, retrain. Every scored prediction was made without access to its answer.
Selection windowThe stretch of history used to choose between rival models. Scores here are optimistic by construction, because the winner was picked for scoring well on them.
HoldoutHistory deliberately set aside and never used for any choice, so the winner's score on it is an honest estimate. Its power erodes each time it is read, which is why this one is now considered spent.
Nested splitAn extra safeguard: train the news scorers on an early slice only, so the judging window is genuinely unseen by them. Without it, models that had memorized their training era won on mirages.
Leakage / lookaheadWhen a model accidentally sees information from after the moment it is predicting. The single most common way backtests lie, and the reason for the iron rule.
MultiplicityTest enough ideas and some will look good by luck alone. With about forty evaluations on one holdout, small positive results should be assumed to be noise until confirmed live.
Standard errorThe uncertainty around a measurement. If two models differ by less than their error bars, they are not distinguishable — a fact this desk prints next to every score.
Winner's curseThe tendency of a contest winner's score to be inflated by luck, so real future performance disappoints. The reason no candidate is trusted on its selection score.

News data

GDELTA free public project that monitors news worldwide and publishes machine-readable summaries. The desk's primary news source, both for daily tone and the article archive.
SlugThe readable tail of a web address, which usually contains the headline. It is what the models actually read — not the full article, an important limitation.
ToneAn automated positivity score for coverage language. Useful in aggregate, unreliable per article, and notoriously bad at sarcasm.
Category / verticalThe six news buckets: NVIDIA, competitors, hyperscalers, AI companies, macro, brokers. The desk tracks each separately because they move the stock differently.
SyndicationThe same wire story republished by many outlets. About 10% of the archive, and it inflates apparent news volume without adding information.
NoveltyHow far today's news sits from the trailing month's average position on the meaning map. High novelty means genuinely new subject matter rather than more of the same.
ConflictHow much same-day articles disagree about direction. The desk's answer to "what if four stories contradict each other" — it measures the disagreement instead of averaging it away.
